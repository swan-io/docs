# Common types of fraud

The common types of fraud introduced on this page can impact individuals and companies, leading to damaged reputations, financial loss, and wasted time for you, your users, and Swan.

While the potential types of fraud seem endless, this page explains certain types to which Swan users might be more vulnerable.
Please review this page thoroughly, including the tips to combat each type of fraud.
Additionally, make sure to read the [fraud protection page](./index.mdx), covering how Swan protects you and your users and how to prevent, react to, and report fraud.
For technical guidance on securing the systems and applications that connect to Swan, see [Secure your integration](../secure-integration/index.mdx).

## Fake advisor and impersonation {#fake-advisor}

In a fake advisor scam, fraudsters **pretend to work for a trusted organization**, such as Swan, your company, a bank, or a security team.
They contact your users by phone, email, text message, or instant messaging, or send them to a fake website.

Their goal is to convince the user to:

- Share confidential information or change their login details.
- Add an account member or a beneficiary.
- Approve a payment.
- Transfer funds to a so-called "secure" account.

Most attempts rely on three mechanisms:

- **Impersonation**: the fraudster uses the organization's name and logo, professional vocabulary, an email address close to the official one, or a [spoofed](#phishing) phone number.
- **False urgency**: the fraudster says a suspicious transaction was detected, the account is about to be blocked, or the funds must be moved immediately. Urgency stops users from checking the request.
- **Trust built on known information**: the fraudster already knows the user's name, company, a recent transaction, or part of their bank details. This information can come from social media, a data breach, or an earlier fraud. It doesn't prove the caller is legitimate.

:::caution What a legitimate institution never asks
Swan, a bank, or any trusted provider **never** asks your users to:

- Share a password, a one-time or multi-factor authentication (MFA) code, a PIN, or a full card number.
- Transfer money to a "secure" account.
- Approve an operation to "cancel" a fraudulent one.
- Install remote-control software or share their screen.
- Hand their card to a courier.
:::

:::warning A validation isn't a verification
Fraudsters often present an operation as a security step.
When users approve a beneficiary, a transfer, or a new device with strong authentication, they confirm the operation shown on their screen.
Strong authentication never turns an outgoing transfer into a cancellation.
:::

:::note Tips to combat fake advisor fraud
- **End the conversation** when a request feels unusual or urgent.
- **Don't trust the phone number or email address displayed**. Both can be spoofed.
- **Contact the organization directly** through a channel you already know, such as the app, the official website, or a saved phone number. Don't use contact details given by the caller.
- **Never approve an operation** you didn't start yourself.
- **Report the attempt quickly**, even if no transaction has been made.
:::

## Account takeover (ATO) {#ato}

ATO fraud occurs when fraudsters gain control of a victim's payment account and use it to perform unauthorized actions.

ATO is usually the **consequence of another fraud**, such as a [fake advisor](#fake-advisor) call, [phishing or vishing](#phishing), a compromised password, or a SIM swap.

### How can an account be compromised? {#ato-how}

Fraudsters can gain access to an account through:

- A fake login page, or a link in an email or text message.
- A phone call asking for an MFA code.
- A password reused across several services.
- A compromised email account linked to the payment account.
- A **SIM swap**, where the fraudster takes over the user's phone number.
- **Malware** on the user's device.
- A session left open on a shared device.
- A **fraudulent new account member or administrator**.

Once connected, fraudsters can change contact details, add members or beneficiaries, make payments, and lock the legitimate user out.
They don't always act right away. Some first study the account and wait for the right moment.

### Warning signs {#ato-warning-signs}

- A login notification the user doesn't recognize.
- A login from a new device or an unusual location.
- A password reset the user didn't request.
- An unexpected change to the email address or phone number.
- A new account member or administrator.
- A new beneficiary.
- An MFA request the user didn't start.
- Suddenly being unable to log in.

:::caution
An account can be compromised **before any fraudulent transaction is visible**.
:::

:::note Tips to combat ATO fraud
- Keep account **login information secure**.
- Use **strong, unique passwords** and passcodes.
- **Don't share account details** by email, phone call, or text message.
- Set up **multi-factor authentication (MFA)** or **two-factor authentication (2FA)** as an extra layer of security in case credentials are compromised.
- Only access the account from **trusted and secure networks**.
- If a password was used on other services, **change it everywhere** it's used.
- If you suspect a **SIM swap**, contact your mobile operator right away.
:::

For the technical controls that protect against ATO in your own interface, including password policies, multi-factor authentication, session timeouts, and token protection, see [Secure your integration](../secure-integration/index.mdx).

## Card {#card}

Those committing card fraud **steal virtual card details**, such as card numbers and card verification values or codes (CVVs or CVCs), or **actual physical cards**.
Then, they use that information to make payments online or by phone, where the purchaser's physical card or presence isn't required.

Types of card fraud include **remote purchase fraud**, **card-not-present (CNP) fraud**, and any activity with **lost**, **stolen**, or **misplaced cards**.

:::caution
A card can be used fraudulently **even while it's still in the cardholder's possession**. Fraudsters only need the card details.
:::

Other common scenarios include:

- **Digital wallet fraud**: the fraudster gets the card added to Apple Pay or Google Pay on their own device, then pays with it.
- **Fraudulent subscriptions**: the fraudster uses the card details to set up a subscription or recurring payments. Amounts are often small to avoid attention.
- **Fake "cancel transaction" requests**: the user receives an email, text message, or call saying a payment must be confirmed or canceled. The fraudster asks for the card details or for a validation presented as a simple check.

:::note Tips to combat card fraud
- Only enter card details on websites with a **secure connection**.
- Only enter card numbers and security codes into **fields designed for those numbers**. Don't enter card details in free-text fields, which are open for any type of text input.
- **Block (cancel) lost or stolen cards immediately**. If you use Swan's Web Banking, send your users the [Support Center article about blocking cards](https://support.swan.io/hc/en-150/articles/18538920070045-Blocking-your-card).
- **Prioritize issuing [single-use virtual cards](../../../topics/cards/virtual/index.mdx#suv)** for non-recurring online payments. These cards are more difficult to use fraudulently.
- **Check small, unusual payments** and **cards added to unknown devices**. A small payment can be a test before larger ones.
:::

## Chief Executive Officer (CEO) {#ceo}

CEO fraud is a cybercrime where individuals impersonate a company's CEO or other top executives.
It's a type of business email compromise (BEC) that results in authorized push payment (APP) fraud.

These individuals send **convincing emails** to company employees. The emails appear to require **immediate, urgent attention**.
In the email, they might ask the employee to transfer funds, provide access to secure portals or documents, or otherwise reveal confidential information about the company.

Consider the following example of a CEO phishing attack:

> Subject: Urgent financial matter (confidential)
>
> Hi Alex,
>
> There's been an urgent development, and we need to pay an invoice immediately.
> I'm in a critical meeting and can't send this transaction myself.
> Could you please handle it right away?
>
> *includes transfer details*
>
> Let me know when it's done.
>
> Thanks,<br />
> Jules<br />
> CEO, MyBrand

Typical requests include:

- An urgent transfer to a new beneficiary.
- Buying gift cards.
- Sending supplier or customer lists, or other confidential documents.
- Bypassing a validation step.

Fraudsters don't only ask for large amounts. They might first **test the company's vigilance with a smaller request**.

:::note Tips to combat CEO fraud
- **Confirm the email address**. Is there a spelling error or typo in the executive's name? Are there extra characters, letters, or numbers in the domain name?
- If you've received other emails from this executive, **does this email look like the others**? Are there slight differences in style, spacing, and voice?
- Even when a request comes from an executive, **never bypass established security measures**.
- **Contact the executive using another established channel**. If you received an email, for example, try calling them or sending them a direct message on the company's messaging platform.
- **Don't trust an existing email thread** by default. If a mailbox is compromised, a fraudulent message can appear in a real conversation.
:::

## Invoice and billing {#invoice-billing}

Invoice and billing fraud involves the use of fake or altered invoices to trick companies.

- **Fake invoice**: fraudulent individuals **send companies fake invoices** that appear to be from the company's regular suppliers. These invoices often **look authentic**, complete with logos and other details. However, the **bank account details** included on the invoice **belong to the fraudulent individuals** rather than a legitimate supplier.
- **Fraudulent IBAN change**: the fraudster imitates a supplier and asks the company to update the supplier's bank details. They often mention a new bank or a reorganization.
- **Diverted genuine invoice**: the fraudster gets a real invoice, **changes only the IBAN**, and sends it back. The amount, supplier, and invoice number are all correct, which makes this hard to detect.

Fraudsters often choose moments when controls are weaker: **holidays**, **accounting close**, **team changes**, **manager absences**, and **busy periods**.

:::caution
Never approve a bank-detail change based on an email alone.
Always verify it through an independent channel you already know, such as a phone number from the supplier's contract.
:::

:::note Tips to combat invoice and billing fraud
- **Establish and respect rigorous processes**, including purchase verification systems, payment validation processes, transaction verification methods, and more.
- Regularly **verify supplier credentials**.
- **Maintain strong, direct lines of communication** with suppliers and contact them directly in case of suspicion.
:::

## Phishing, vishing, smishing, and spoofing {#phishing}

Phishing, vishing, smishing, and spoofing involve fraudulent individuals **impersonating legitimate organizations**, such as companies or governmental agencies, contacting you by email, phone, or text message.
All four are used in authorized push payment (APP) fraud.

- **Phishing** → email
- **Vishing** → phone call
- **Smishing** → text message
- **Spoofing** → falsified sender identity, such as a fake caller ID, a lookalike domain, or a fake display name

They attempt to **steal sensitive information**, including user names and passwords, government ID numbers, financial information, and more.
These emails, phone calls, and text messages can be **quite convincing**.
For example, email and text messages often link to websites that look almost identical to an organization's real website.

### Techniques are often combined {#combined}

Fraudsters rarely use one technique alone. A typical combined attack looks like this:

1. The user receives a text message about a suspicious transaction.
1. They click a link and enter some information.
1. A few minutes later, a fake advisor calls them, sometimes from a spoofed number.
1. The caller already knows the user's name and some account details.
1. They ask the user to approve an operation or share a code.
1. They use this access to take over the account or steal funds.

:::caution The fraudster may already have your information
Data breaches regularly affect telecom operators, government services, travel agencies, and retailers.
Fraudsters use this data to choose victims and build convincing stories.
For example, they pretend to be Amazon about an order, Netflix about a subscription, SNCF about a ticket or refund, or a telecom operator about a phone line.
Knowing accurate details doesn't prove someone is legitimate.
:::

### QR code fraud {#qr-code}

A QR code can lead to a **fake login page**, a **fraudulent payment page**, a **fake activation process**, or a **malicious download**.
Treat an unexpected QR code like a link in an email or text message: don't scan it without checking where it came from.

:::warning A real fraudulent transaction can be used to gain your trust
Some frauds happen in two stages:

1. The fraudster steals card details, for example through phishing or a fake merchant site, and makes one or more small payments.
1. They call the victim and describe those payments exactly: amount, merchant, time, and the card's last digits. The victim checks their account, sees the payments, and believes the caller is a real advisor.

The fraudster then asks for a code, a new beneficiary, or a transfer to a "secure" account.
:::

:::tip Key reflex
A real fraudulent transaction confirms that a transaction is suspicious. **It never confirms the identity of the caller.**
Hang up and contact the organization through a channel you already know.
:::

:::note Tips to combat phishing, vishing, smishing, and spoofing fraud
- Don't provide information or funds **urgently**.
- **Contact the organization directly**. Use a different communication channel to ask them about the suspicious message. For example, if they contacted you by phone, send an email. If you received a text message, call them.
- **Check links before you open them**. Fraudulent websites often use small URL changes to impersonate legitimate sites. [Double-check](../../../partnership/protections/fraud/#double-check) for any spelling errors, typos, added characters, letters, or numbers in the link.
- **Open websites directly** in your browser instead of clicking links included in an email or text message.
- **Don't scan unexpected QR codes**.
- Enable **multi-factor authentication (MFA)** or **two-factor authentication (2FA)** for added security if your credentials are compromised.

For technical guidance on protecting credentials, tokens, and redirect flows in your integration, see [Secure your integration](../secure-integration/index.mdx).
:::

## Authorized push payment (APP) fraud: investment and romance scams {#app}

In these scams, fraudsters don't take control of the account.
Instead, they **manipulate users into making the payment themselves**, often after building trust over weeks or months.

### Investment scams {#investment}

Fraudsters offer an opportunity presented as **highly profitable**, **risk-free**, exclusive, or available for a limited time.
They might start with a small amount to gain trust, then show a **fake dashboard with fictitious gains** to encourage larger investments.
When the victim tries to withdraw, the fraudster asks for **withdrawal fees, taxes, or "unlocking" fees**.

### Romance scams {#romance}

Fraudsters build an **emotional relationship** with the victim, often online. They usually:

- Move the relationship forward very quickly.
- Refuse video calls or meetings in person.
- Describe a sudden emergency, such as medical, legal, or travel costs.
- Ask for money, with requests that grow over time.
- Ask the victim to keep the relationship or the requests secret.

:::note Tips to combat APP fraud
- **Never transfer money to a "secure" account**, or to an account that doesn't belong to the person you're dealing with.
- **Never pay fees to unlock a withdrawal** or to recover money you already sent.
- **Verify the beneficiary's identity** before any transfer.
- **Talk to someone you trust** before sending money.
:::
