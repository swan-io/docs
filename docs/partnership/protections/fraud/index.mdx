# Fraud protection

Fraud attacks grow more complex every day.
It's important to know how Swan works to keep you and your users safe, as well as how to prevent, react to, and report suspicions of fraud.

Review the page covering [common types of fraud](./types.mdx) to understand the types of fraud your users might face.

Technical security controls are another important part of fraud prevention. See [Secure your integration](../secure-integration/index.mdx) for guidance on protecting credentials, tokens, sessions, APIs, webhooks, and data in your own systems.

### Prevent {#prevent}

Preventing fraud is a critical responsibility shared by you, your users, and Swan.
Follow these tips to strengthen your product integration and help your users avoid falling victim to fraud.

#### 🧠 Know what fraud looks like. {#educate}

Fraud often looks close to the real thing, but not quite.
Any time a communication feels suspicious, evaluate it closely, applying the actions described in this section.

Learn about different types of fraud and how they're attempted.
Review the page covering [common types of fraud](./types.mdx), and stay informed about new ways fraudulent individuals are trying to trick people.

#### ⏳ Don't respond urgently. {#urgency}

Fraudulent individuals try to pressure you and your users into believing that their request is urgent and must happen immediately.
Don't fall for it.
Instead, follow established processes. Don't let fraudulent individuals convince you to bypass processes due to urgency.

#### 💬 Use a secondary communication channel. {#comm-channel}

If you're concerned about a request, it's never a bad idea to confirm with the person or organization who sent the request in another way.
Don't hesitate to contact them through another communication channel.

If they called you, email them.
If they emailed you, call or text them.
You could also contact them on a social media platform you know they use and control.

#### 🔎 Double-check. {#double-check}

If a request feels suspicious, it never hurts to double-check.
Check for typos or errors in names, websites, email addresses, and messages.

Fraudulent websites often make small changes to the URL to appear legitimate. 
You can easily miss these changes. 

- **Check for missing letters**, especially in site names. 
For example, `swn.io` instead of `swan.io`.
- **Identify lookalike letters or characters**, such as a lowercase “l” instead of an uppercase “I”, or a “0” instead of an “O”. 
For example, `swan.i0` instead of `swan.io`.
- **Check for extra characters**, such as hyphens or added symbols in domain names.
For example, `sw-an.io` or `swan-io.com` instead of `swan.io`.
- **Spot incorrect domain endings**. Look for an incorrect suffix, such as `.com` or `.org`, in place of the correct `.io`. 
For example, `swan.com` instead of `swan.io`.

:::tip Check the URL
When requests include a link, always check the full URL character by character. 
Before you open the link, hover over it and look at the bottom of your screen to make sure it's real and safe. 
If you're unsure, open a new tab or window and search for the website yourself instead of using the provided link.
:::

If you receive a phone call, consider whether it sounds authentic.

#### 🔐 Protect account information. {#protect-info}

For the technical requirements that apply to your own interface, including password storage, multi-factor authentication, and session timeouts, see [Secure your integration](../secure-integration/index.mdx).

You and your users have a lot of control around protecting your account information.
Use unique passwords and passcodes every time.
Store them in a secure password manager.

Consider making multi-factor authentication (MFA) or two-factor authentication (2FA) mandatory for your product, and activate it for personal use for any app you can.
Help your users understand that activating MFA or 2FA is like adding a second lock to a door, for which the key is a one-time password texted to you or a code from a secure app.
While MFA and 2FA add an extra step to logging in, they increase online safety significantly.

Pairing strong password hygiene with MFA or 2FA keeps your information much safer.

Never share login information using insecure channels, like text message or email.
If you need to share login information with someone you trust, use your password manager's built-in sharing feature.

Encourage your users to protect their cards and payments, too:

- **Use [single-use virtual cards](../../../topics/cards/virtual/index.mdx#suv)** for one-off online payments where possible.
- **Turn on payment notifications** when available, so they notice unexpected payments quickly.
- **Check small, unusual payments**. Fraudsters often test a card with a small amount first.
- **Review subscriptions and saved cards** on merchant accounts regularly.
- **Check new beneficiaries, devices, and logins**, and report anything they don't recognize.

#### 📢 Make your official channels easy to recognize. {#official-channels}

Fraudsters often pretend to be you or Swan.
Help your users tell real messages from fake ones:

- **List your official** email addresses, websites, apps, and support channels in a place users can find quickly.
- Remind users to **never use contact details from a suspicious message**. They should contact you through a channel they already know.
- Explain that a **phone number shown on screen doesn't prove** who's calling. It can be spoofed.
- Remind users that your teams **never ask for passwords, MFA codes, or transfers to a "secure" account**.

#### 📣 Run awareness campaigns tailored to your users. {#awareness}

Prevention messages are more effective when they match your users and the way they use your product.

- **Use examples from your sector**, and mention brands and services your users deal with, such as Amazon, Netflix, SNCF, or telecom operators.
- **Step up your messages** during holidays and busy periods, after a data breach in the news, or when a new fraud pattern appears.
- **Use the channels your users already check**: in-app messages, banners, help articles, emails, and notifications.
- Use **simple, clear language** that doesn't assume technical or financial expertise.

For scenarios to include in your campaigns, see [Common types of fraud](./types.mdx).

#### 🙋 Make reporting easy. {#easy-reporting}

The sooner a user reports fraud, the more you and Swan can do to limit the damage.

- Make your **reporting channels easy to find**, and explain what information to share.
- Encourage users to **report attempts even when they didn't lose money**, such as clicking a suspicious link.
- Ask users to **keep evidence**: emails, text messages, screenshots, and phone numbers.
- Use a **reassuring tone**. Users who think they made a mistake should feel safe asking for help.

#### ☑️ Stay up-to-date {#update}

Keep the software on your computers, phones, and other devices up to date.
Software providers regularly improve built-in safety measures, so take the time to install updates.

Review Swan's [Changelog](/changelog/) regularly and update your integration accordingly so your product remains secure.

:::note
Putting these tips into practice can help you and your users to prevent fraud as online fraudulent activity becomes increasingly prevalent.
:::

### React {#react}

If you or your users suspect fraud, **please react immediately**.
Remember, Swan never contacts you or your users to transfer money outside of established Support channels.
As a Swan Partner, you also shouldn't contact your users to transfer money.

:::caution Communication channels
If a request feels urgent, your users should contact the requester using a second, previously known communication channel.
For example, if your user **receives an email request**, they should **call the requester** using a phone number they already have—not a phone number provided in the original email.
:::

Swan invites you and your users to **be SAFE, not sorry** (borrowing from the [Yale University Information Security Office](https://cybersecurity.yale.edu/)).
Encourage the following behavior from your users, and practice it yourselves.

| Action | Explanation |
| :---: | --- |
| **`S`**<br />**See something suspicious?** | Pay attention to how you feel about a request, and encourage your users to pay attention, too.<br /><br /><ul><li>Do you feel **fearful** or **scared**?</li><li>Is the requester pressuring you to act **urgently** or **quickly**?</li><li>Does the request feel **questionable** or **suspicious**?</li></ul>If the answer to any of these questions is yes, **take a step back** before responding. **Trust how you feel**. |
| **`A`**<br />**Act quickly** | [Report](#report) the incident to Swan right away. Don't wait, don't be embarrassed, and don't let someone talk you out of your gut feeling about the incident.<br /><br />However, don't respond quickly to the request. Wait to transfer funds or provide information until it's confirmed to be a legitimate request. |
| **`F`**<br />**Follow instructions** | Provide Swan with all requested information as quickly as possible. Stay actively responsive as Swan follows up on the incident. |
| **`E`**<br />**Exercise discretion** | Stay cautious until you know the account is secure. [Reset passcodes](https://support.swan.io/hc/en-150/articles/20531243310749), limit [account membership permissions](../../../topics/accounts/memberships/index.mdx#permissions), and do anything else you feel might protect you until the incident is resolved. |

#### 🧑‍💼 Secure, collect, transmit. {#support-team}

When a user reports fraud, your support team's priority is to **prevent any further loss**.
Your support team is the first point of contact, but they don't need to investigate.
Follow three steps:

1. **Secure**. Ask the user to:
    - Stop replying to the fraudster, clicking their links, and approving operations.
    - Change their passwords from official websites or apps, and secure the email address linked to their account.
    - [Block](../../../topics/cards/physical/guide-block-unblock.mdx) or cancel their card if it's compromised.
    - Turn on MFA or 2FA when available.
1. **Collect** the essentials: who's affected, what happened, and when. Note what the user shared, the transactions or cards involved, and any evidence.
1. **Transmit** the case to Swan right away. Don't wait for a complete file. See [Report](#report).

:::caution
Your support team must **never ask users for** their password, MFA code, PIN, or full card number.
:::

For a one-page summary, see the [Fraud quick-reference card](./quick-reference.mdx).

### Report a new fraud pattern or campaign {#new-pattern}

Alert Swan as soon as you notice:

- A new fraud scenario.
- A phone number, email address, domain, or link used in several fraud attempts.
- Someone impersonating Swan, you, or a known brand, including with a fake website.
- Several reports with elements in common.
- An unusual increase in fraud-related requests from your users.

**Don't wait** for several victims or for confirmation that it's a campaign.
A new pattern or a doubt is enough.

Submit a [Swan Support request](https://partner-support.swan.io/hc/en-gb/requests/new) with the facts you know, without trying to qualify the incident yourself:

- What happened, and when it started.
- How many cases you know of, and which users seem affected.
- What the cases have in common.
- Any losses observed.
- Actions you've already taken.
- Evidence you have, such as screenshots, messages, phone numbers, and links.

### When Swan issues a fraud alert {#swan-alert}

When Swan confirms a fraud campaign or a new risk, Swan might ask you to help protect your users.
Swan shares the validated scenario, the users concerned, the messages to send, and the recommended channels.

When you receive an alert:

- **Relay the messages Swan validated** through the channels your users already check.
- **Brief your support teams**.
- **Target the users concerned** when possible.
- **Send Swan any new reports** linked to the campaign.

:::caution
- Don't reword messages in a way that changes their meaning.
- Don't speculate about the origin or scale of the incident.
- Don't communicate publicly without Swan's approval.
:::

### What happens after a report {#after-report}

After you report fraud, Swan investigates the case.
Your support team can explain the next steps to users, but must **never promise a refund or the recovery of funds**.

#### Recall {#recall}

A recall is a request Swan sends to the beneficiary's bank to try to recover funds after a transfer.
It's an **attempt to recover funds, not an automatic cancellation**.

Whether a recall succeeds depends on the payment's status, the beneficiary's bank, and whether the funds are still available.
It might also need the agreement of the beneficiary or their bank.
Launching a recall isn't a refund decision.

#### Refund {#refund}

Reporting fraud or launching a recall **doesn't automatically mean the user is refunded**.
Swan analyzes each case and considers factors such as:

- Whether the user authorized it, and how it was authenticated.
- Any technical or operational error.
- The security measures in place.
- How quickly the fraud was reported.
- The information and evidence available.

#### Information Swan can't always share {#confidentiality}

Swan can't always share every detail of an investigation.
This is because of banking confidentiality, investigation secrecy, other users' privacy, security requirements, and regulatory obligations.

For example, Swan might not be able to share:

- Full details of the analysis.
- Information about other accounts.
- Internal detection rules, or what triggered an alert.
- Exchanges with a beneficiary bank, an acquirer, or authorities.
